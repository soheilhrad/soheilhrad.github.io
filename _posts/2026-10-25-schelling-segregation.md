Many of us have the same feeling: the world is dividing. In politics, in culture, between countries, sometimes even inside a team at work. I don't know how much of that feeling can be measured. But one model keeps coming back to me when I try to understand it.

In 1971, Thomas Schelling published a model of how segregation can arise from individual choices (Schelling, 1971). Picture a board with two kinds of coins for two groups, and some empty squares. A coin's neighbours are the eight squares around it. A coin is content as long as at least half of its neighbours are like it. If not, it moves to the nearest empty square where it would be content.

So every coin accepts up to half of its neighbours being different. Yet in one of Schelling's worked examples, the average person ends up in a neighbourhood that is 90% their own colour, counting themselves. Nobody asked for 90%.

How does that happen? In Schelling's words: "Nobody in this model anticipates the movements of others." Each move changes the neighbourhood of someone else, and that can set off the next move. What stays with me is how ordinary each step is. One coin moves because it feels a little out of place, and its move changes what its neighbours see, so someone else moves. No one plans a divide, and no one needs to.

## Four ideas, in plain words

These four ideas become concrete in a model like this. The plain-words versions are mine, not Schelling's.

- **Adaptation:** each person adjusts to the people around them.
- **Emergence:** a pattern appears in the whole group that none of its members was aiming for.
- **Systems:** one person's move changes what the next person sees, so our choices are linked.
- **Unintended consequences:** results nobody asked for, like 90% alike from a rule that allowed half different.

When a society splits, it is worth looking at the system as well as the people in it.

## Why it stays with me

Part of this is psychological. Belonging matters to us, and part of who we are comes from who we belong with. It feels easier among people who understand us without explanation, and harder in rooms where we keep explaining ourselves. Some of those moves come from comfort, some from biases we haven't noticed. Each can make sense to the person making it.

Online, the loop can run faster. Feeds learn from what we click and show us more of the same, so what we chose yesterday shapes what we see today. I don't know how much of our division comes from that, and I hold the idea loosely.

Then there is the largest scale. When countries and alliances seem to sort into "us" and "them", many forces are at work: history, power, economics, strategy. A board of coins explains none of that.

## What a model can and cannot show

A model like this shows that a simple rule can be enough to produce strong separation. It does not show that this is why any real neighbourhood, feed or alliance is divided. Schelling himself writes in his abstract: "Inferences about individual motives can usually not be drawn from aggregate patterns." He also lists other causes of segregation, such as the practices of organisations.

What the board gives me is a question: how much of a divide is designed, and how much builds up from many people each doing what feels safe?

Schelling's model is one I have always loved, and it inspired the design of my own agent-based model. I use it as a way of seeing, not as an explanation of everything.

So when the world looks divided, I try to ask a second question next to "who did this?": what small moves are building it, and which of them might be mine?

And if small moves can pull us apart, I wonder whether moves of the same size can also bring us back.

## Try this

Draw a grid, leave some squares empty, and place two kinds of coins. Move any coin with fewer than half of its neighbours like it to an empty square where it would be content. Or run it online: the NetLogo Segregation model (Wilensky, 1997).
